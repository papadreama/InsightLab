## Philosophical Perspectives on Wonder

- Wonder has been a subject of philosophical inquiry since antiquity.  **Ancient Greek philosophers viewed wonder as the beginning of philosophy.** **Plato emphasized wonder as a quest for truth, achieved by embracing holistic perception and remaining open to uncertainty.** He considered this experience inherently valuable, regardless of any logical outcomes.  **Conversely, Aristotle viewed curiosity as a quest for information seeking to reduce uncertainty and rationally deconstruct a phenomenon into parts.**
- **During the rise of scientific inquiry in early modern Europe, wonder was dismissed, due to its association with "speechlessness and a kind of paralysis".**  **This dismissal stemmed from the identification of wonder with what the sources call 'deep wonder'** **and the belief that it hindered the progress of learning.**
- **In contrast to curiosity, which seeks to resolve uncertainty and acquire specific knowledge, deep or contemplative wonder is characterized by a sense of mystery and an acceptance of the limits of human understanding.**  **This type of wonder, exemplified by the philosophical pondering of existence itself, does not seek definitive answers but rather inspires a sense of awe and a desire to contemplate the unknown.**
- **The sources highlight the educational importance of deep wonder, suggesting that it fosters a deeper appreciation for the world and encourages attentiveness to the unknown.** **Wonder inspires a longing for meaning and can even lead to a love of the world.** **This emphasis on openness to experience and embracing uncertainty contrasts with the pursuit of knowledge solely for the sake of reducing uncertainty.**

## Philosophical Approaches to Wonder

- **Philosophical approaches to wonder often explore its nature, its relationship to other concepts like curiosity and awe, and its role in human experience.**  **Some philosophers, such as William Desmond, distinguish different forms of wonder.**  **Desmond, for example, identifies astonishment, perplexity, and curiosity as forms of wonder.**  **His approach challenges the strict separation of wonder and curiosity, emphasizing their interconnectedness.**
- **Other philosophical approaches, such as those presented by Anders Schinkel and Sophia Vasalou, delve into the educational importance of wonder, particularly deep wonder.**  **They argue that deep wonder, despite its association with speechlessness, is crucial for fostering a sense of the world's mystery and for encouraging a lifelong love of learning.** **They emphasize the need for educational systems to create space for experiences of wonder, allowing students to develop an appreciation for the unknown and to cultivate an enduring curiosity about the world.**
- **Some philosophical discussions of wonder also touch upon its ethical implications.**  **The capacity for wonder may be seen as connected to a sense of humility and a recognition of the inherent value of the world and its inhabitants.**  **This perspective suggests that wonder can contribute to the development of ethical awareness and a sense of responsibility towards the environment and others.**  **However, the sources do not explore this connection in detail.**

The philosophical exploration of wonder invites us to consider the value of embracing uncertainty and appreciating the world's inherent mystery. By fostering a sense of wonder, both in ourselves and in others, we can cultivate a deeper understanding of our place in the universe and a lifelong passion for exploration and discovery.

## Schinkel on the Educational Importance of Deep Wonder

Schinkel argues that **deep wonder is fundamentally important to education**, even though it may seem counterintuitive because it can render us speechless and does not lead to a straightforward quest for answers. He acknowledges that **wonder is a complex phenomenon** and that there are different types of wonder.

### Active Wonder vs. Deep Wonder

Schinkel contrasts two main types of wonder:

- **Active Wonder**: Like curiosity, it involves a drive to explore, investigate, and explain. It is motivated by a "not-yet-knowing" and seeks to resolve that state by finding answers and gaining knowledge.
- **Deep Wonder**: It is characterized by a sense of mystery, an acceptance that some things may never be fully understood, and an appreciation of the inherent wonder of the object, even without a full explanation.

Schinkel suggests that while active wonder is an obvious ally of education, **deep wonder may appear to be in tension with education**, especially in today's results-oriented educational climate, because it does not readily translate into measurable outcomes. He argues, however, that this view is too simplistic.

### Deep Wonder as a Foundation for Education

Schinkel contends that **deep wonder is crucial to education** because it:

- **Cultivates an appreciation for the world**: Deep wonder encourages us to attend to things for their own sake, fostering a love of the world. This openness to the world, Schinkel suggests, is at the heart of what it means to be educated.
- **Enhances our understanding of the limits of knowledge**: The experience of deep wonder highlights what we don't know, what we only think we know, and what may ultimately be unknowable. This "refined ignorance", Schinkel posits, is an important component of being educated.

Schinkel draws an analogy between **deep wonder and love**, specifically a love for the "Other" in another person, to illuminate the educational value of deep wonder. He points out that, just as love can inspire us to understand the other person more fully, deep wonder can motivate a quest for understanding, even if that understanding can never fully encompass the mystery of the object of wonder.

### Deep Wonder Does Not Preclude a Quest for Understanding

Schinkel clarifies that while deep wonder may temporarily render us speechless, it doesn't permanently block speech or efforts at articulation. He suggests that **deep wonder can actually motivate us to express**, in ever new and imperfect ways, why the object of our wonder is worthy of attention.

He acknowledges that **deep wonder is not easily reconciled with the focus on measurable outcomes** that characterizes many contemporary educational systems. However, he suggests that we should resist the temptation to dismiss deep wonder as irrelevant to education. Instead, he believes we should **seek ways to create space for deep wonder in education** so that we can nurture the love of the world and the refined ignorance that are essential to the educational project.

### Key Points to Remember

- Schinkel emphasizes the distinction between **active wonder** (seeking explanations) and **deep wonder** (appreciating mystery).
- He argues that **deep wonder plays a crucial role in education** by fostering a love of the world and an awareness of the limits of knowledge.
- He uses the **analogy of love** to highlight how deep wonder can inspire a quest for understanding, even if that understanding remains incomplete.
- He suggests that **educators should prioritize creating space for deep wonder** in educational settings.

## Connecting Wonder to Beginner's Mind

The sources provided do not directly discuss the Buddhist concept of beginner's mind. However, they do explore various aspects of wonder, particularly deep wonder, that share significant similarities with the core principles of beginner's mind.

**Beginner's mind**, also known as _shoshin_ in Zen Buddhism, emphasizes approaching experiences with an open, receptive, and unprejudiced mind, as if encountering them for the first time. It involves letting go of preconceived notions, judgments, and expectations, allowing for a fresh and unbiased perception of reality.

**Deep wonder**, as described in the sources, shares a similar spirit of openness and receptivity. It involves a recognition of the inherent mystery and complexity of the world, even in the face of existing knowledge.

Here are some key connections between wonder, particularly deep wonder, and beginner's mind:

- **Appreciation of the "That" rather than the "How":** Both deep wonder and beginner's mind encourage a shift in focus from seeking explanations and analyzing how things work to simply appreciating the fact that they exist. This resonates with Schinkel's point about deep wonder being focused on the "that" rather than the "how", leading to an appreciation of the inherent wonder of the object itself. Similarly, beginner's mind encourages us to see things as they are, without the filter of our preconceptions and interpretations.
- **Openness to the Unknown:** Both concepts embrace the idea that true understanding often arises from acknowledging the limits of our knowledge. Deep wonder arises from a sense of mystery and an acceptance that some things may never be fully grasped. Beginner's mind also encourages us to approach experiences as if we know nothing, allowing for new insights and perspectives to emerge. This connects with Schinkel's argument that deep wonder helps us cultivate a "refined ignorance," recognizing what we don't know and what may be ultimately unknowable.
- **Non-Judgmental Observation:** Both deep wonder and beginner's mind emphasize the importance of approaching experiences without judgment. In deep wonder, we are captivated by the object of wonder, suspending our critical faculties to appreciate its inherent qualities. Similarly, beginner's mind encourages us to observe experiences with a clear and unbiased mind, free from the distortions of our opinions and biases.
- **Cultivating a Sense of Awe:** While the sources distinguish between wonder and awe, they also acknowledge that the two concepts are closely related. Beginner's mind, with its emphasis on openness and receptivity, can be seen as a pathway to cultivating a sense of awe, a profound emotional response to something vast and incomprehensible. This aligns with the suggestion in the sources that awe can inspire a sense of humility and a willingness to "let shine".

It is worth noting that the sources focus on the cognitive and emotional dimensions of wonder, while the concept of beginner's mind also has ethical and spiritual implications within the context of Buddhist practice. However, the shared emphasis on openness, receptivity, and a willingness to embrace the unknown suggests a strong connection between deep wonder and beginner's mind.

While the sources do not offer specific guidance on how to cultivate wonder within a Buddhist framework, the principles of beginner's mind, such as mindfulness and meditation, could be seen as practices that foster the kind of open and receptive mindset that is conducive to experiencing wonder.
